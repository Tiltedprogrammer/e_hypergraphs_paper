\chapter{Enriched Category Theory}\label{chapter:enriched}
Our aim is to give a categorical interpretation to a fragment of e-graphs, from which we obtain both generalisations of e-graphs and a justification of the congruences that arise in them.
At the ground level, an e-graph represents many equivalent terms of a language.
As we saw in the previous chapter, the terms of a language (modulo equations) can be seen as morphism in a category with finite products.
In general, however, this view does not distinguish between equivalent terms, which is essential for e-graphs since it is desirable to pick the best term out of a family of equivalent ones.
Thus, for a categorical reading, we work with a free theory $\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}$ without any equations imposed.
% There is a well-known categorical interpretation of terms of a language as morphisms in a category with finite products introduced by Lawvere~\cite{LawvereFunctorialSemanticsAlgebraic1963}.
The missing ingredient then is the categorical interpretation of a term (a morphism) denoting multiple equivalent terms (parallel morphisms) simultaneously.
This amounts to extra structure on the hom-set and is where we appeal to enriched category theory.

Enriched categories were introduced by Eilenberg and Kelly~\cite{EilenbergKellyClosedCategories} as a generalisation of ordinary categories in which hom-sets (ignoring the size issues) are replaced by objects of a monoidal category $\mathcal{V}$, called the base of enrichment. 
An early striking example of enrichment is given by generalised metric spaces, each of which may be seen as a category enriched over $([0,\infty], \ge, +, 0)$---the extended non-negative reals with the reversed order, with addition as the monoidal product and $0$ as the unit.
In addition to allowing for a more general formulation of metric spaces, this view provided categorical insights into the theory of metric spaces and fed ideas back into category theory~\cite{lawvere_metric_1973}.
In this thesis the base for enrichment is the category of join-semilattices, which we argue is a natural setting for interpreting several equivalent terms simultaneously.
None of the general category theory in this chapter is new, but the particular case study using this base is novel. 
Our purpose is to assemble standard results---enriched categories and their monoidal, closed, and change-of-base structures---for this specific base, and to record the concrete form each takes for join-semilattices, since that is what the later chapters rely on.
We assume familiarity with basic category theory and monoidal categories.

\paragraph{Notation.}
We use standard categorical notation: $\mathcal{C}$ denotes a category, $A,B,C$
its objects and $f,g,h$ its morphisms, with $\obj{\mathcal{C}}$ for the
collection of objects and $\mathcal{C}(A,B)$ for the hom-set of morphisms
$A \to B$. 
We will also occasionally use $\mor{\mathcal{C}}$ for the collection of morphisms of $\mathcal{C}$.
Composition is written $g \circ f$ (for $f : A \to B$ and
$g : B \to C$) and the identity on $A$ is $\id_A$. We write $\defeq$ for
``is defined to be'', and set category names in sans serif, as in
$\catname{Set}$ and $\catname{SLat}$.
For a monoidal category we write $\otimes$ for the tensor product, of objects
or---as $f \otimes g$---of morphisms, $\I$ for the unit, $\alpha,\lambda,\rho$
for the associator and the left and right unitors, and $\sym$ for the symmetry.
To distinguish enriched categories from ordinary ones, where it matters, we
write $\enriched{C}$, and $\enriched{C}(A,B)$ for its hom-object (an object of
the base $\mathcal{V}$ rather than a set).
Finally, given functors $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$, we denote the fact that $F$ is left adjoint to $G$ by writing $F,G : \mathcal{C} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{D} \quad F \dashv G$.
The rest of the notation will be defined in-place as needed.

We start with the definition of the category of semilattices.

\section{Category of Semilattices}

\begin{definition}[Semilattice]\label{def:slat}
	A \textit{semilattice} $(S,\join)$ is a set $S$ equipped with a binary operation $\join : S \times S \to S$ satisfying the following properties for all $a,b,c \in S$:
    \begin{align*}
        a \join (b \join c) &\equiv (a \join b) \join c \quad \text{(associativity)}\\
        a \join b &\equiv b \join a \quad \text{(commutativity)}\\
        a \join a &\equiv a \quad \text{(idempotence)}
    \end{align*}
\end{definition}

We will use semilattices to model e-classes of our generalised e-graphs, with $a \join b \join c$ intuitively representing an e-class with three terms $a$, $b$, and $c$.
We do not require a unit (bottom element) for $\join$.
A unit $u$ would satisfy $a \join b \join c \join u = a \join b \join c$ for every e-class, so $u$ would be a term belonging to every e-class of a given type---equivalently, a term equivalent to every other term of that type---and no term plays this role. 
Requiring a unit would moreover force every hom-semilattice to contain $u$, hence to be non-empty, outlawing empty hom-objects. 
Yet an empty hom-semilattice is perfectly sensible: it is just an empty hom-set, the hom of two objects with no terms between them, exactly as an ordinary category may have empty hom-sets.
Semilattices that are equipped with a unit are sometimes called \textit{bounded}, i.e., they are idempotent commutative monoids.

\begin{remark}
We have defined semilattices algebraically, as sets with an
associative, commutative, idempotent operation $\join$. This is
equivalent to the order-theoretic definition of a \emph{join-semilattice}
as a poset in which every pair of elements has a least upper bound:
given the algebraic structure, the relation
\[
  x \le y \quad\defeq\quad x \join y = y
\]
is a partial order in which $x \join y$ is the least upper bound of
$\{x, y\}$; conversely, the binary least upper bound of any such poset is
associative, commutative and idempotent. We stress that the algebraic
presentation is \emph{self-dual}: it does not by itself single out an
order, and reading $\join$ as a meet rather than a join would induce the
opposite order $x \le y \defeq x \join y = x$. Throughout this thesis we
read $\join$ as a join, so that $x \le y \iff x \join y = y$ and $x \join y$
is the least upper bound.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
A typical example of a (unbounded) semilattice is the set of all non-empty subsets of a given set $X$ with $\join \defeq \cup$.
\end{example}
\begin{definition}[Semilattice homomorphism]
	A homomorphism between two semilattices $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ is a map $\phi : S_1 \to S_2$ such that $\phi(s \join_{S_{1}} s') = \phi(s) \join_{S_{2}} \phi(s')$ for all $s,s' \in S_{1}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Category of semilattices]
	Semilattices with their respective homomorphisms form a category that we denote $\catname{SLat}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    This is the same data as the category $\catname{Alg}(\Sigma_{\catname{SLat}}, \mathcal{E})$ for $\Sigma_{\catname{SLat}}$ given by a single operator $\Sigma_{\catname{SLat}}(2) = \{ \join \}$ and 
    \begin{align*}
    \mathcal{E} = \{
    x \join (y \join z) &\equiv (x \join y) \join z,\\
    x \join y &\equiv y \join x,\\
    x \join x &\equiv x\}~.
    \end{align*}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:slat-forgetful}
The forgetful functor $U_{\catname{SLat}} : \catname{SLat} \to \catname{Set}$ sends a
semilattice $(S,\join)$ to its underlying set $S$ and a homomorphism to
its underlying function.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:slat-forgetful-representable}
$U_{\catname{SLat}}$ is representable: writing $\I_{\catname{SLat}} = \{*\}$ for the
one-element semilattice, there is a bijection
\[
  \catname{SLat}(\I_{\catname{SLat}}, S) \;\cong\; U_{\catname{SLat}}(S),
  \qquad h \longmapsto h(*),
\]
natural in $S$. It is injective since $\I_{\catname{SLat}}$ has a single
element, and surjective because for any $s \in S$ the assignment
$* \mapsto s$ is a homomorphism: the only equation to check is
$s \join s = s$, which holds by idempotence. Thus
$U_{\catname{SLat}} \cong \catname{SLat}(\I_{\catname{SLat}}, -)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}
The category $\catname{SLat}$ is complete and cocomplete and $U_{\catname{SLat}}$ creates limits.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\catname{SLat} \cong \catname{Alg}(\Sigma_{\catname{SLat}}, \mathcal{E})$, whose signature has a single operation of finite arity.
Categories of algebras for a finitary signature are complete and cocomplete, with limits created by the forgetful functor; see, for example~\cite[Thm. 3.4.5, Prop. 3.4.1]{Borceux_1994}.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}\label{remark:slat-limits}

A similar reasoning shows that $\I_{\catname{SLat}}$ is the terminal object in $\catname{SLat}$, since the one-element set $\{*\}$ is the terminal object in $\catname{Set}$.
\end{remark}

Colimits, however, are a different story: the result above proves that they exist, but gives no easy recipe to construct them.
We will not be interested in arbitrary colimits, but we will need to understand what form the coproducts have for what follows.

\begin{definition}[(Arbitrary) Coproduct of semilattices]
    Let $(S_i)_{i \in X}$ be semilattices, indexed by some set $X$, and let $G \defeq \bigcoprod_{i \in X} U(S_i)$ be the disjoint union of their underlying sets, writing $\kappa_i : U(S_i) \to G$ for the injections.
    Consider formal joins
    \[
    x_1 \join \ldots \join x_n \qquad n \geq 1,
    \]
    of elements $x_i$ drawn from $G$.
    We define $\bigcoprod_{i \in X} S_i$ to be the semilattice consisting of the formal joins above, quotiented by the congruence $\equiv$ generated by associativity, commutativity, idempotence and by the following family of relations:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\kappa_i(s_1 \join_{S_i} s_2) \sim \kappa_i(s_1) \join \kappa_i(s_2), \qquad s_1, s_2 \in S_i, i \in X$,
    \end{itemize}
    treating $\join$ on the right as a formal symbol and $\join_{S_i}$ as an operation in $S_i$.
    Given $[x]$ and $[y]$, equivalence classes of formal joins, we define $[x] \join [y] \defeq [x \join y]$.
    Injections $\iota_i : S_i \to \bigcoprod_{i \in X} S_i$ are then given by sending $s \mapsto [\kappa_i(s)]$, i.e., sending to the equivalence classes induced by $\equiv$.
    The family of relations above ensures that $\iota_i$ are homomorphisms.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
Let $(S_i)_{i \in X}$ be semilattices.
Given any semilattice $Q$ together with homomorphisms $j_i : S_i \to Q$, there is a unique homomorphism $u : \bigcoprod_{i \in X} S_i \to Q$ such that $u \circ \iota_i = j_i$ for all $i$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $j : G \to U_{\catname{SLat}}(Q)$ be the copairing $[U_{\catname{SLat}}(j_i)]_{i \in X}$ for $U_{\catname{SLat}}(j_i) : U_{\catname{SLat}}(S_i) \to U_{\catname{SLat}}(Q)$, that is $j(\kappa_i(s)) = j_i(s)$.
Define a function $\hat{u}$ on formal joins by
\[
\hat{u}(x_1 \join \ldots \join x_n) \defeq j(x_1) \join_Q \ldots \join_Q j(x_n).
\]
By construction,
\[
\hat{u}(x \join y) = \hat{u}(x) \join_Q \hat{u}(y)\tag{$\ast$}
\]
for all formal joins $x$ and $y$.
Let $\theta \defeq \{(x,y) \mid \hat{u}(x) = \hat{u}(y)\}$ be a relation on formal joins.
This is an equivalence relation, and moreover a congruence: if $x \theta x'$ and $y \theta y'$, then by $(\ast)$ we have
\begin{align*}
\hat{u}(x \join y) &= \hat{u}(x) \join_Q \hat{u}(y)\\
&= \hat{u}(x') \join_Q \hat{u}(y')\\
&= \hat{u}(x' \join y').
\end{align*}
Finally, $\theta$ contains the relations generating $\equiv$: associativity, commutativity and idempotence are respected because $\join_Q$ is associative, commutative and idempotent, and $\sim$ is respected by the following chain:
\begin{align*}
\hat{u}(\kappa_i(s_1) \join \kappa_i(s_2)) &= j(\kappa_i(s_1)) \join_Q j(\kappa_i(s_2))\\
&= j_i(s_1) \join_Q j_i(s_2)\\
&= j_i(s_1 \join_{S_i} s_2)\\
&= j(\kappa_i(s_1 \join_{S_i} s_2))\\ 
&= \hat{u}(\kappa_i(s_1 \join_{S_i} s_2)),
\end{align*}
by definition of $j$ and because $j_i$ is a homomorphism.
Since $\equiv$ is the smallest congruence containing the generating relations, we have $\equiv \subseteq \theta$, and hence $\hat{u}$ respects $\equiv$.
We may therefore define
\[
u([x]) \defeq \hat{u}(x)~.
\]

We now check that $u$ is a homomorphism satisfying $u \circ \iota_i = j_i$ for all $i$.
For the former we have
\begin{align*}
u([x] \join [y]) &= u([x \join y])\\
&= \hat{u}(x \join y)\\
&= \hat{u}(x) \join_{Q} \hat{u}(y)\\
&= u([x]) \join_Q u([y])~.
\end{align*}
For the latter, we have
\[
(u \circ \iota_i)(s) = u([\kappa_i(s)]) = \hat{u}(\kappa_i(s)) = j(\kappa_i(s)) = j_i(s)~, 
\]
for every $s \in S_i$.

Now, every element of $\bigcoprod_{i \in X} S_i$ is the class of a formal join
$x_1 \join \ldots \join x_n$ with each $x_k = \kappa_{i_k}(s_k)$ for some $i_k \in X$ and $s_k \in S_{i_k}$,
and hence
\[
  [x_1 \join \ldots \join x_n]
  = [\kappa_{i_1}(s_1)] \join \ldots \join [\kappa_{i_n}(s_n)]
  = \iota_{i_1}(s_1) \join \ldots \join \iota_{i_n}(s_n).
\]
Let $u'$ be any homomorphism with $u' \circ \iota_i = j_i$ for all $i$.
Then
\[
  u'\big([x_1 \join \ldots \join x_n]\big)
  = u'\big(\iota_{i_1}(s_1)\big) \join_{Q} \ldots \join_{Q}
    u'\big(\iota_{i_n}(s_n)\big)
  = j_{i_1}(s_1) \join_{Q} \ldots \join_{Q} j_{i_n}(s_n),
\]
using that $u'$ is a homomorphism and $u' \circ \iota_i = j_i$. 
The right-hand side is $u([x_1 \join \ldots \join x_n])$, so $u' = u$.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}
In the case when each $S_i = \I_{\catname{SLat}}$, the relations $\sim$ reduce to instances of idempotence, since each $S_i$ is a singleton.
Hence $\bigcoprod_{i \in X} S_i$ is the set of all finite non-empty subsets of $X$ under union.
\end{corollary}

The following construction is not specific to $\catname{SLat}$, but holds for any cocomplete monoidal category $\mathcal{V}$.
\begin{proposition}[Prop. 6.4.6~\cite{Borceux_1994}]\label{prop:cocomplete_monoidal_free}
    Let $\mathcal{V} = (\mathcal{V}, \otimes, \I_{\mathcal{V}})$ be a locally small cocomplete monoidal category.
    Let $U_{\mathcal{V}} : \mathcal{V} \to \catname{Set}$ be the forgetful functor given by $U_{\mathcal{V}}(X) = \mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, X)$.
    Then the latter has a left adjoint free functor $F_{\mathcal{V}} : \catname{Set} \to \mathcal{V}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	The functor $F_{\mathcal{V}}$ is defined by letting $F_{\mathcal{V}}(A) = \bigcoprod_{A} \I_{\mathcal{V}}$, the coproduct of $A$-many copies of the monoidal unit of $\mathcal{V}$.
	The adjunction is then given by the following natural isomorphisms
	\begin{align*}
		\mathcal{V}(\bigcoprod_{A}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}), B) & \cong \prod_{A}(\mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, B))       \\
		                                                   & \cong \catname{Set}(A,\mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, B)) \\
		                                                   & \cong \catname{Set}(A, U_{\mathcal{V}}(B))
	\end{align*}
	The first isomorphism is given by the definition of the colimit.
	The second is the isomorphism $\prod_{A} X \cong \catname{Set}(A, X)$ established by the Yoneda lemma:
	\[
	\catname{Set}\big(Y, \textstyle\prod_A X\big) \cong \prod_A \catname{Set}(Y, X) \cong \catname{Set}\big(\textstyle\bigcoprod_A Y, X\big) \cong \catname{Set}(Y \times A, X) \cong \catname{Set}\big(Y, \catname{Set}(A,X)\big),
	\]
	using, in order, the universal property of the product, that of the coproduct, and currying.
    The last bijection is the representability of $U_{\mathcal{V}}$.
	As all three bijections are natural in $A$ and $B$, their composite exhibits $F_{\mathcal{V}} \dashv U_{\mathcal{V}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:cocomplete_monoidal_free}
    For $\mathcal{V}$ a closed cocomplete \emph{symmetric} monoidal category, the free functor $F_{\mathcal{V}} : \catname{Set} \to \mathcal{V}$ is symmetric strong monoidal as a functor $(\catname{Set}, \times) \to (\mathcal{V}, \otimes)$.
\end{corollary}

	We have

	naturally in $A$ and $B$, where we used that $- \otimes X$ and $X \otimes -$ preserve colimits, being left adjoints by the symmetry and monoidal closedness of $\mathcal{V}$.

    We also have that

First note that $F_{\mathcal{V}}(f)$ for $f : X \to Y$ is the morphism $\bigcoprod_{X} \I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \bigcoprod_{Y} \I_{\mathcal{V}}$, i.e., a map out of a coproduct.

The latter is given by the injection $\iota_{f(x)} : \I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \bigcoprod_{Y} \I_{\mathcal{V}}$.
Second, let the isomorphism on the left be $\Phi_{A,B}$ and the one on the right be $\Phi_{B,A}$.
Every isomorphism in the chain given by $\Phi_{A,B}$ and $\Phi_{B,A}$ is given by the universal property of the coproduct and hence maps injections to injections.

is given by coproduct $\bigcoprod_{A} \zeta$ and we have $\theta_2 \circ j_{a} = j'_{a} \circ \zeta$.
Then $\theta_3$ is given by the $\id$, since $\bigcoprod_{B}(\I_{\mathcal{V}} \otimes \I_{\mathcal{V}})$ and $\bigcoprod_{B} \I_{\mathcal{V}}$ give the same cocone, suppressing the unitors.
The last map $\theta_4$ is such that $\theta_4 \circ (j'_{a} \circ \beta_{b}) = \iota_{(a,b)}$---the nested family $(j'_{a} \circ \beta_{b})_{a,b}$ is a universal cocone over the discrete diagram indexed by $A \times B$.
Writing $\iota_{a} \otimes \kappa_{b}$ as
$(\iota_{a} \otimes \id_{F_{\mathcal{V}}(B)}) \circ (\id_{\I_{\mathcal{V}}} \otimes \kappa_{b})$ exposes an
injection at the first step, and tracing through the chain gives
\begin{align*}
\Phi_{A,B} \circ (\iota_{a} \otimes \kappa_{b}) &= \theta_4 \circ \theta_3 \circ \theta_2 \circ \theta_1 \circ (\iota_{a} \otimes \id_{F_{\mathcal{V}}(B)}) \circ (\id_{\I_{\mathcal{V}}} \otimes \kappa_{b})\\
&= \theta_4 \circ \theta_3 \circ \theta_2 \circ j_{a} \circ (\id_{\I_{\mathcal{V}}} \otimes \kappa_{b})\\
&= \theta_4 \circ \theta_3 \circ j'_{a} \circ \zeta \circ (\id_{\I_{\mathcal{V}}} \otimes \kappa_{b})\\
&= \theta_4 \circ \theta_3 \circ j'_{a} \circ \beta_{b}\\
&= \theta_4 \circ j'_{a} \circ \beta_{b}\\
&= \iota_{(a,b)}~.
\end{align*}

Since both paths are maps out of a coproduct, it suffices to check that they agree after
precomposition with $\iota_{a} \otimes \kappa_{b}$.
Tracing down and then right gives
$F_{\mathcal{V}}(\sym_{A,B}) \circ \Phi_{A,B} \circ (\iota_{a} \otimes \kappa_{b}) = F_{\mathcal{V}}(\sym_{A,B}) \circ \iota_{(a,b)} = \iota_{(b,a)}$ by the two facts above.
Tracing right and then down, naturality of $\sym$ in both variables applied to $\iota_{a}$ and
$\kappa_{b}$, together with
$\sym_{\I_{\mathcal{V}}, \I_{\mathcal{V}}} = \id$, gives
$\sym_{F_{\mathcal{V}}(A),F_{\mathcal{V}}(B)} \circ (\iota_{a} \otimes \kappa_{b}) = \kappa_{b} \otimes \iota_{a}$, hence
$\Phi_{B,A} \circ (\kappa_{b} \otimes \iota_{a}) = \iota_{(b,a)}$.
The two composites therefore agree.
The associativity and unit coherences for $F$ are established by the same tracing.
\end{proof}
\begin{definition}\label{def:slat-tensor}
We define a tensor product of semilattices $S_1$ and $S_2$ as follows.
$S_1 \otimes S_2$ is the free semilattice on the set $S_1 \times S_2$---finite non-empty formal joins of pairs $(s_1,s_2)$ with $s_{1} \in S_{1}$, $s_2 \in S_{2}$---quotiented by the following bilinearity relations
\begin{itemize}
    \item $(s_1 \join_{S_{1}} s_1', s_2) \equiv (s_1,s_2) \join_{S_{1} \otimes S_{2}} (s'_1, s_2)$
    \item $(s_1, s_2 \join_{S_{2}} s'_2) \equiv (s_1, s_2) \join_{S_{1} \otimes S_{2}} (s_1,s'_2)$
\end{itemize}
Writing $\eta_{S_1,S_2} : S_1 \times S_2 \to S_1 \otimes S_2$, $(s_1,s_2) \mapsto [(s_1,s_2)]$, for the inclusion of pairs, the tensor product is characterised by the universal property that every \emph{bimorphism} out of $S_1 \times S_2$---a function $b : S_1 \times S_2 \to T$ that is a homomorphism in each variable separately---factors as $b = \bar{b} \circ \eta_{S_1,S_2}$ for a unique homomorphism $\bar{b} : S_1 \otimes S_2 \to T$.
On morphisms, given $f : S_1 \to T_1$ and $g : S_2 \to T_2$, the composite $\eta_{T_1,T_2} \circ (f \times g)$ is a bimorphism, and we define $f \otimes g : S_1 \otimes S_2 \to T_1 \otimes T_2$ to be the unique homomorphism with
\[
(f \otimes g) \circ \eta_{S_1,S_2} = \eta_{T_1,T_2} \circ (f \times g).
\]
The unit for this tensor product is $\I_{\catname{SLat}} = \{*\}$---a one-element semilattice.
\end{definition}


\begin{remark}\label{rem:slat-tensor-functorial}
The action of $\otimes$ on morphisms is functorial: $\id \otimes \id = \id$ and
\[
(f \otimes g) \circ (h \otimes k) = (f \circ h) \otimes (g \circ k).
\]
Both equations follow from the uniqueness in the universal property, since each side restricts along $\eta$ to the same bimorphism; for the second,
\[
\begin{aligned}
(f \otimes g) \circ (h \otimes k) \circ \eta
&= (f \otimes g) \circ \eta \circ (h \times k) \\
&= \eta \circ (f \times g) \circ (h \times k) \\
&= \eta \circ \big((f \circ h) \times (g \circ k)\big),
\end{aligned}
\]
which is exactly the bimorphism defining $(f \circ h) \otimes (g \circ k)$. 
By the same reasoning the unitors $S \otimes \I_{\catname{SLat}} \cong S$ (given by $(s,*) \mapsto s$), the associators and the symmetry are the unique homomorphisms induced by the evident bimorphisms, and the coherence axioms hold because the corresponding diagrams already commute at the level of bimorphisms, where they reduce to coherence for the cartesian product in $\catname{Set}$. 
This equips $\catname{SLat}$ with a symmetric monoidal structure.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{remark:slat-cartesian-product}
    The above definition of a tensor product should not be confused with a tensor given by the binary product $\times$, which is constructed in the same vein as the tensor product above with the quotienting
    \[
        (s_1,s_2) \join_{S_1 \times S_2} (s_1',s_2') \equiv (s_1 \join_{S_1} s_1', s_2 \join_{S_2} s_2')~.
    \]
    The reason for using the former construction is that it has better theoretical properties, in particular, the free semilattice functor is strong monoidal (\cref{cor:slat-free-monoidal}).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:slat-internal-hom}
For semilattices $S_1$ and $S_2$, the \emph{internal hom} $[S_1, S_2]$ is the set $\catname{SLat}(S_1, S_2)$ of homomorphisms $S_1 \to S_2$, made into a semilattice by the pointwise join
\[
(f \join g)(x) \defeq f(x) \join g(x).
\]
This is well-defined, as $f \join g$ is again a homomorphism:
\begin{align*}
    (f \join g)(x \join y) &= f(x \join y) \join g(x \join y)\\
    &= f(x) \join f(y) \join g(x) \join g(y)\\
    &= f(x) \join g(x) \join f(y) \join g(y)\\
    &= (f \join g)(x) \join (f \join g)(y).
\end{align*}
\end{definition}
  \begin{proposition}\label{prop:slat-smcc}
    $\catname{SLat}$ is a closed symmetric monoidal category.
  \end{proposition}
  \begin{proof}
    The symmetric monoidal structure is given by the tensor product of \cref{def:slat-tensor}, with functoriality and coherence established in \cref{rem:slat-tensor-functorial}.
    For closedness, take the internal hom $[S_2, T]$ of \cref{def:slat-internal-hom}; the universal property of the tensor gives bijections, natural in $S_1$ and $T$,
    \[
    \catname{SLat}(S_1 \otimes S_2, T) \;\cong\; \{\text{bimorphisms } S_1 \times S_2 \to T\} \;\cong\; \catname{SLat}(S_1, [S_2, T]),
    \]
    where the first is the universal property of $\otimes$ and the second is currying: a bimorphism $b : S_1 \times S_2 \to T$ corresponds to the homomorphism $s_1 \mapsto b(s_1, -)$ into $[S_2, T]$.
    Hence $- \otimes S_2 \dashv [S_2, -]$, so $\catname{SLat}$ is closed.
  \end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:slat-free-monoidal}
    Specialising~\cref{prop:cocomplete_monoidal_free} and~\cref{cor:cocomplete_monoidal_free} to $\mathcal{V} = \catname{SLat}$, with the latter being cocomplete category with the closed symmetric monoidal structure as per~\cref{prop:slat-smcc}, we get the adjunction
    \[
    F_{\catname{SLat}}, U_{\catname{SLat}} : \catname{Set} \rightleftarrows \catname{SLat} \qquad F_{\catname{SLat}} \dashv U_{\catname{SLat}}~,
    \]
    such that the functor $F_{\catname{SLat}}$ is symmetric strong monoidal.
\end{corollary}
\begin{remark}\label{rem:slat-free-concrete}
Concretely, $F_{\catname{SLat}}(A)$ is the semilattice of non-empty finite subsets of $A$ under
union, the injection $\iota_{a}$ picks out the singleton $\{a\}$, and $F_{\catname{SLat}}(f)$
sends a subset to its image, so $F_{\catname{SLat}}(f)(\{x\}) = \{f(x)\}$.
Writing $\Phi_{A,B}$ for the isomorphism
$F_{\catname{SLat}}(A) \otimes F_{\catname{SLat}}(B) \cong F_{\catname{SLat}}(A \times B)$, the
morphism $\iota_{a} \otimes \kappa_{b}$ picks out the element $[(\{a\},\{b\})]$---the class of
the pair of singletons---and
\[
\Phi_{A,B}\big([(S,T)]\big) = S \times T, \qquad \text{in particular} \qquad
\Phi_{A,B}\big([(\{a\},\{b\})]\big) = \{(a,b)\}~.
\]
Thus the tensor of two free semilattices is free on the product of the generating sets.
This is what distinguishes it from the cartesian product of the same two semilattices, since
\[
\left|F_{\catname{SLat}}(A) \otimes F_{\catname{SLat}}(B)\right| = 2^{|A||B|}-1,
\qquad
\left|F_{\catname{SLat}}(A) \times_{\catname{SLat}} F_{\catname{SLat}}(B)\right|
= (2^{|A|}-1)(2^{|B|}-1)~.
\]
\end{remark}
All of the above properties of $\catname{SLat}$ are essential to use it as a base for enrichment.

\begin{example}
A classical example of an enriched category is a preorder viewed as a category enriched over the poset $\catname{2} = (0 \le 1)$, regarded as a category with two objects $0$ and $1$, a single non-identity morphism $0 \to 1$ (and no morphism $1 \to 0$), and tensor given by $\wedge$ with unit $1$.
We get that $A \le B$ if $\enriched{C}(A,B)$ is given by the object $1$ of $\catname{2}$ and $A \not \le B$ if $\enriched{C}(A,B)$ is given by the object $0$.
Reflexivity follows from the unit morphism $j_A : 1 \to \enriched{C}(A,A)$, which forces $\enriched{C}(A,A) = 1$, that is $A \le A$.
The composition $(B \le C) \circ (A \le B)$ is given by a morphism $1 \wedge 1 \to \enriched{C}(A,C)$.
Since $1 \wedge 1 = 1$ and the only morphism out of $1$ is the identity, it must be the case that $\enriched{C}(A,C) = 1$; the composite of $A \le B$ and $B \le C$ thus yields $A \le C$, which is transitivity of $\le$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Ordinary categories are recovered as categories enriched over $\catname{Set}$ with the cartesian product as the monoidal structure.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Categories enriched over $\catname{SLat}$ ($\catname{SLat}$-categories) are categories in which hom-objects are semilattices and composition and identities are homomorphisms of semilattices.
The latter, in particular, means that given $f \join g \in \enriched{C}(A,B)$ and $h \join k \in \enriched{C}(B,C)$, we have
\[
(h \join k) \circ (f \join g) = h \circ f \join h \circ g \join k \circ f \join k \circ g.

\begin{remark}
We can verify that $\enriched{M} \times \enriched{N}$ is indeed a product $\mathcal{V}$-category by establishing an isomorphism natural in $\enriched{L}$,
\[
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat}(\enriched{L}, \enriched{M} \times \enriched{N}) \cong \mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat}(\enriched{L}, \enriched{M}) \times_{\catname{Cat}} \mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat}(\enriched{L}, \enriched{N}),
\]
recalling that $\mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat}$ is a 2-category and therefore the hom-objects are objects of $\catname{Cat}$---the product in the latter is the usual componentwise one.

The forward direction is given by postcomposition with $\mathcal{V}$-functors $\Pi_1 : \enriched{M} \times \enriched{N} \to \enriched{M}$ and $\Pi_2 : \enriched{M} \times \enriched{N} \to \enriched{N}$, which act on objects by applying the corresponding projection for the product in $\catname{Set}$ and on hom-objects by applying the corresponding projection of the product in $\mathcal{V}$;
their $\mathcal{V}$-functoriality is then guaranteed by the universal property of the product in $\mathcal{V}$ and the definition of composition and identities in $\enriched{M} \times \enriched{N}$.
Naturality in $\enriched{L}$ then follows because composition of $\mathcal{V}$-functors is associative.
The reverse direction of the isomorphism follows by defining a $\mathcal{V}$-functor $\langle G_1, G_2 \rangle$ for $G_1 : \enriched{L} \to \enriched{M}$ and $G_2 : \enriched{L} \to \enriched{N}$ acting on objects by pairing the corresponding object-level functions of $G_1$ and $G_2$ and acting on hom-objects by $\langle (G_1)_{X,Y}, (G_2)_{X,Y} \rangle$ in $\mathcal{V}$.
Since $\circ_{\enriched{M} \times \enriched{N}}$ and $j^{\enriched{M} \times \enriched{N}}_{(A,B)}$ are themselves defined
componentwise, joint monicity of the product projections in $\mathcal{V}$ reduces the
$\mathcal{V}$-functor axioms for $\langle G_1, G_2 \rangle$ to those for $G_1$ and $G_2$;
the same argument shows the two assignments are mutually inverse.
On $2$-cells the argument is identical.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
\label{def:monoidal_enriched_category}
    Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a symmetric monoidal category and let
    $\enriched{C}$ be a $\mathcal{V}$-category.
    A monoidal structure on $\enriched{C}$ consists of $\mathcal{V}$-functors
    \[
        \enriched{C} \boxtimes \enriched{C}
        \xrightarrow{\enriched{\otimes}}
        \enriched{C}
        \qquad
        \enriched{I}
        \xrightarrow{I_{\enriched{C}}}
        \enriched{C},
    \]
    together with invertible $\mathcal{V}$-natural transformations whose
    components are the associators and unitors
    \[
        a_{A,B,C} :
        (A \enriched{\otimes} B) \enriched{\otimes} C
        \to
        A \enriched{\otimes} (B \enriched{\otimes} C),
        \qquad
        \ell_A : I_{\enriched{C}} \enriched{\otimes} A \to A,
        \qquad
        r_A : A \enriched{\otimes} I_{\enriched{C}} \to A,
    \]
    satisfying the pentagon and triangle axioms. Here $\enriched{I}$ is
    the unit $\mathcal{V}$-category defined above, and the unit functor
    selects the object $I_{\enriched{C}}$ of $\enriched{C}$.

    A symmetric monoidal $\mathcal{V}$-category is additionally equipped
    with an invertible $\mathcal{V}$-natural transformation
    \[
        \gamma :
        \enriched{\otimes}
        \Rightarrow
        \enriched{\otimes} \circ
        \mathfrak{s}_{\enriched{C},\enriched{C}},
    \]
    with components
    \[
        \gamma_{A,B} :
        A \enriched{\otimes} B
        \to
        B \enriched{\otimes} A,
    \]
    satisfying the usual hexagon axioms and the symmetry equation
    \[
        \gamma_{B,A} \circ \gamma_{A,B}
        =
        \id_{A \enriched{\otimes} B}.
    \]

    The definition of composition in

\begin{example}\label{ex:slat_tensor}
    For an $\catname{SLat}$-category $\enriched{C}$, the hom-objects of
    $\enriched{C} \boxtimes \enriched{C}$ are tensor products of
    semilattices.
    This, together with the tensor being a homomorphism of semilattices, means that we have the following property for the tensor product
    \[
    (f \join g) \otimes h = f \otimes h \join g \otimes h,
    \]
    because in a tensor product of semilattices we have
    $(f \join g, h) \equiv (f,h) \join (g,h)$, so the action of the
    tensor product functor must agree on both sides.

    The familiar bifunctoriality property of the tensor product takes the following form in an $\catname{SLat}$-category:
    \begin{align*}
    &((f_1 \join g_1) \otimes (h_1 \join k_1)) \circ ((f_2 \join g_2) \otimes (h_2 \join k_2))\\
    &= ((f_1 \otimes h_1) \join (f_1 \otimes k_1) \join (g_1 \otimes h_1) \join (g_1 \otimes k_1))\\
    &\qquad\qquad\circ ((f_2 \otimes h_2) \join (f_2 \otimes k_2) \join (g_2 \otimes h_2) \join (g_2 \otimes k_2))\\
    &= ((f_1 \otimes h_1) \circ (f_2 \otimes h_2)) \join \cdots \join ((g_1 \otimes k_1) \circ (g_2 \otimes k_2))\\
    &= ((f_1 \circ f_2) \otimes (h_1 \circ h_2)) \join \cdots \join ((g_1 \circ g_2) \otimes (k_1 \circ k_2))\\
    &= ((f_1 \circ f_2) \join (f_1 \circ g_2) \join (g_1 \circ f_2) \join (g_1 \circ g_2))\\
    &\qquad\qquad\otimes ((h_1 \circ h_2) \join (h_1 \circ k_2) \join (k_1 \circ h_2) \join (k_1 \circ k_2))\\
    &= ((f_1 \join g_1) \circ (f_2 \join g_2)) \otimes ((h_1 \join k_1) \circ (h_2 \join k_2))~.
    \end{align*}
\end{example}

Given an ordinary $\catname{Set}$-enriched category $\mathcal{C}$, it is possible to turn it into an $\catname{SLat}$-enriched category $\enriched{C}$ in a canonical way, which is an instance of a more general translation defined below.
Such a construction is practically useful for our purposes: given a category $\mathcal{C}$, in which morphisms characterise some terms of interest, we can turn it into an $\catname{SLat}$-category $\enriched{C}$, where we get the ability to express families of equivalent terms using the structure of a semilattice, i.e., model e-classes categorically.

\begin{definition}[Change of base. Proposition 6.4.7~\cite{Borceux_1994}]\label{def:change_of_base}
    Let $\mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{W}$ be monoidal categories and let $F : \mathcal{V} \to \mathcal{W}$ be a (strong) monoidal functor.
    It induces a $2$-functor $\widetilde{F} : \mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat} \to \mathcal{W}\text{-}\catname{Cat}$ defined as follows.
    \paragraph{Objects.} Given a $\mathcal{V}$-category $\enriched{C}$, $\widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})$ has the same objects as $\enriched{C}$.
    \paragraph{Hom-objects.} For any pair of objects $A,B$, $\widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(A,B) \defeq F(\enriched{C}(A,B))$.
    \paragraph{Composition.} For any triple of objects $A,B,C$, the composition morphism $\circ_{A,B,C} : \widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(B,C) \otimes \widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(A,B) \to \widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(A,C)$ is given by the following composition
    % https://q.uiver.app/#q=WzAsNCxbMCwwLCJcXHRpbGRle0Z9KFxcZW5yaWNoZWR7Q30pKEIsQykgXFxvdGltZXMgXFx0aWxkZXtGfShcXGVucmljaGVke0N9KShBLEIpIl0sWzAsMiwiRihcXGVucmljaGVke0N9KEIsQykpIFxcb3RpbWVzIEYoXFxlbnJpY2hlZHtDfShBLEIpKSJdLFsyLDIsIkYoXFxlbnJpY2hlZHtDfShCLEMpIFxcb3RpbWVzIFxcZW5yaWNoZWR7Q30oQSxCKSkiXSxbNCwyLCJGKFxcZW5yaWNoZWR7Q30oQSxDKSkgPSBcXHRpbGRle0Z9KFxcZW5yaWNoZWR7Q30pKEEsQykiXSxbMCwxLCI9IiwyXSxbMSwyLCJcXGNvbmciXSxbMiwzLCJGKFxcY2lyYykiXV0=
\[
\adjustbox{width=\textwidth}{
\begin{tikzcd}
	{\widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(B,C) \otimes \widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(A,B)} &&&& \\
	\\
	{F(\enriched{C}(B,C)) \otimes F(\enriched{C}(A,B))} && {F(\enriched{C}(B,C) \otimes \enriched{C}(A,B))} && {F(\enriched{C}(A,C)) = \tilde{F}(\enriched{C})(A,C)}
	\arrow["{=}"', from=1-1, to=3-1]
	\arrow["\cong", from=3-1, to=3-3]
	\arrow["{F(\circ)}", from=3-3, to=3-5]
\end{tikzcd}}
\]
\paragraph{Identities.} For any object $A$, the identity morphism $j_{A} : \I_{\mathcal{W}} \to \widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(A,A)$ is given by the following composition
\[
\I_{\mathcal{W}} \xrightarrow{\cong} F(\I_{\mathcal{V}}) \xrightarrow{F(j_{A})} F(\enriched{C}(A,A)) = \widetilde{F}(\enriched{C})(A,A)
\]
\paragraph{Functors.} Given a $\mathcal{V}$-functor $G : \enriched{C} \to \enriched{D}$, $\widetilde{F}(G) : \widetilde{F}(\enriched{C}) \to \widetilde{F}(\enriched{D})$ is given by the same mapping on objects and the following morphism on hom-objects
\[
(\widetilde{F}\enriched{C})(A,B) = F(\enriched{C}(A,B)) \xrightarrow{F(G_{A,B})} F(\enriched{D}(GA,GB)) = (\widetilde{F}\enriched{D})(GA,GB)
\]
\paragraph{Natural transformations.} Given a $\mathcal{V}$-natural transformation $\alpha : G \to H$ between $\mathcal{V}$-functors $G,H : \enriched{C} \to \enriched{D}$, $\widetilde{F}(\alpha) : \widetilde{F}(G) \to \widetilde{F}(H)$ is given by the following family of morphisms
\[
\I_{\mathcal{W}} \xrightarrow{\cong} F(\I_{\mathcal{V}}) \xrightarrow{F(\alpha_{A})} F(\enriched{D}(GA,HA)) = (\widetilde{F}\enriched{D})(GA,HA)
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Strictly speaking, change-of-base construction requires only that $F$ be lax monoidal, with the isomorphism above replaced by the obvious morphisms.
Since in our case functor $F$ is strong monoidal, we write down the definition with this stronger requirement instead.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
    Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a usual $\catname{Set}$-category and let $F_{\catname{SLat}} : \catname{Set} \to \catname{SLat}$ be the free semilattice functor of~\cref{cor:slat-free-monoidal}.
    We let the change-of-base $2$-functor $\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}$ be the change-of-base functor induced by $F_{\catname{SLat}}$.
    Then $\enriched{C} = \widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})$ has the same objects as $\mathcal{C}$ and every hom-object $\enriched{C}(A,B)$ additionally contains all finite joins of morphisms in $\mathcal{C}(A,B)$ quotiented by the axioms of a semilattice, or, equivalently, consists of all finite non-empty subsets of $\mathcal{C}(A,B)$.
    The identity $j_{A}$ is given by the identity of $\mathcal{C}(A,A)$ and the composition is defined elementwise.
    $\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}$ being a $2$-functor means that $\enriched{C}$ inherits all adjunctions from $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{example}
\begin{remark}
    When the functor $\widetilde{F}$ is induced by a free functor from $\catname{Set}$, we call it a free enrichment.
    For a functor $F_{\mathcal{V}} : \catname{Set} \to \mathcal{V}$, we will label the change-of-base $2$-functor as $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}$.
\end{remark}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:change-of-base-op}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be an ordinary category and let $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}$ be a change-of-base $2$-functor induced by a symmetric strong monoidal functor $F_{\mathcal{V}} : \catname{Set} \to \mathcal{V}$.
Then, we have that $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}} = \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}})$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The categories agree on objects.
The hom-objects of $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}})$ are given by 
\[
(\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}))(A,B) = F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(A,B)) = F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(B,A))~.
\]
The hom-objects of $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}}$ are given by 
\[
(\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}})(A,B) = (\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}))(B,A) = F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(B,A))~.
\]
Therefore, they also agree on hom-objects.
We need to check if the composition and identities agree.
For any triple of objects $A,B,C$, we have that the following diagram commutes
\[\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=small]
	&& {(\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}})(B,C) \otimes (\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}})(A,B)} \\
	{(\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}})(A,B) \otimes (\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}})(B,C)} && {(\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}))(B,C) \otimes (\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}))(A,B)} \\
	{\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})(B,A) \otimes \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})(C,B)} && {F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(B,C)) \otimes F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(A,B))} \\
	{F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(B,A)) \otimes F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(C,B))} && {F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(B,C) \times \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(A,B))} \\
	{F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(B,A) \times \mathcal{C}(C,B))} && {F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(A,C))} \\
	{\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(C,A))} && {F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(C,A))}
	\arrow["{\id \otimes \id}"', from=1-3, to=2-1]
	\arrow["{=}"{description}, draw=none, from=1-3, to=2-3]
	\arrow["{\sym_{\mathcal{V}}}"', from=2-1, to=3-1]
	\arrow["{=}"{description}, draw=none, from=2-3, to=3-3]
	\arrow["{=}"{description}, draw=none, from=3-1, to=4-1]
	\arrow["{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(B,C),\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(A,B)}}", from=3-3, to=4-3]
	\arrow["{\Phi_{\mathcal{C}(B,A),\mathcal{C}(C,B)}}"', from=4-1, to=5-1]
	\arrow["{F_{\mathcal{V}}(\circ_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}})}", from=4-3, to=5-3]
	\arrow["{F_{\mathcal{V}}(\circ_{\mathcal{C}})}"', from=5-1, to=6-1]
	\arrow["{=}"{description}, draw=none, from=5-3, to=6-3]
	\arrow["{=}"{description}, draw=none, from=6-1, to=6-3]
\end{tikzcd}~.\]
First note that $\circ_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}} = \circ_{\mathcal{C}} \circ \sym_{\catname{Set}}$, thus the equality between the left and right paths amounts to 
\[
F_{\mathcal{V}}(\circ_{\mathcal{C}}) \circ F_{\mathcal{V}}(\sym_{\catname{Set}}) \circ \Phi_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(B,C),\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(A,B)} = F_{\mathcal{V}}(\circ_{\mathcal{C}}) \circ \Phi_{\mathcal{C}(B,A),\mathcal{C}(C,B)} \circ \sym_{\mathcal{V}}
\] 
and we have that 
\[
F_{\mathcal{V}}(\sym_{\catname{Set}}) \circ \Phi_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(B,C),\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}(A,B)} = \Phi_{\mathcal{C}(B,A),\mathcal{C}(C,B)} \circ \sym_{\mathcal{V}}~,
\] 
because the functor $F_{\mathcal{V}}$ is symmetric monoidal, as established in~\cref{cor:cocomplete_monoidal_free}.
Identity maps agree by the definition of $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})^{\mathrm{op}}$.
\end{proof}
\begin{proposition}[Theorem 5.7.1~\cite{cruttwell2008normed}]\label{prop:tensor_preserved_by_enrichment}
    Let $\mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{W}$ be symmetric monoidal categories and let $F : \mathcal{V} \to \mathcal{W}$ be a symmetric (lax) monoidal functor.
    Then, the induced $2$-functor $\widetilde{F} : \mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat} \to \mathcal{W}\text{-}\catname{Cat}$ maps (symmetric) monoidal $\mathcal{V}$-categories to (symmetric) monoidal $\mathcal{W}$-categories.
\end{proposition}


Along with free enrichment it is equally useful to consider the functor induced by the right adjoint to $F_{\mathcal{V}} : \catname{Set} \to \mathcal{V}$, given by $U_{\mathcal{V}} : \mathcal{V} \to \catname{Set}$, $U_{\mathcal{V}} \defeq \mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}},-)$.
\begin{definition}\label{def:underlying_category}
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a locally small monoidal category and
let $\enriched{C}$ be a $\mathcal{V}$-category.
The underlying category $\mathcal{C}$ of $\enriched{C}$ has the same objects as $\enriched{C}$ with morphisms $f \in \mathcal{C}(A,B)$ given by $\mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, \enriched{C}(A,B))$, that is, by a morphism $\I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{C}(A,B)$.
Composition of $\I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{C}(A,B)$ and $\I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{C}(B,C)$ is given by the composite
\[
\I_{\mathcal{V}} \cong \I_{\mathcal{V}} \otimes \I_{\mathcal{V}} \xrightarrow{g \otimes f} \enriched{C}(B,C) \otimes \enriched{C}(A,B) \xrightarrow{\circ_{A,B,C}} \enriched{C}(A,C)~.
\]
Identities are given by those of $\enriched{C}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{remark:U_lax_monoidal}
The functor $U_{\mathcal{V}} : \mathcal{V} \to \catname{Set}$ is lax
monoidal~\cite[Ex. 2.1.11]{cruttwell2008normed}, with structure maps
\[
\mathfrak{1}_{\catname{Set}} \to U_{\mathcal{V}}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}),\ * \mapsto

\qquad
U_{\mathcal{V}}(X) \times U_{\mathcal{V}}(Y) \to U_{\mathcal{V}}(X \otimes Y),\
(f,g) \mapsto (f \otimes g) \circ \lambda_{\I_{\mathcal{V}}}^{-1}~,
\]
these being precisely the composites used in~\cref{def:underlying_category}.
It is lax symmetric monoidal whenever $\mathcal{V}$ is
symmetric~\cite[Prop. 5.2.2]{cruttwell2008normed}.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[2-adjunction $\catname{Cat} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat}${\cite[Sec. 2.5]{Kelly2022BASICCO}}]\label{prop:2-adjunction}
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a locally small symmetric monoidal closed cocomplete category, and let $F_{\mathcal{V}}, U_{\mathcal{V}} : \catname{Set} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{V}$, $F_{\mathcal{V}} \dashv U_{\mathcal{V}}$,
be the adjunction of~\cref{prop:cocomplete_monoidal_free}.
Since $F_{\mathcal{V}}$ is strong monoidal and $U_{\mathcal{V}}$ is lax monoidal, both induce $2$-functors by change of base,
\[
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}, \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}} : \catname{Cat} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat},
\]
where $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}$ is the underlying-category construction
of~\cref{def:underlying_category}.
These form a 2-adjunction $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}} \dashv \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}$: there is an isomorphism of
categories $\Xi_{\mathcal{L}, \enriched{B}}$
\[
\mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat}(\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}, \enriched{B})
\;\cong\;
\catname{Cat}(\mathcal{L}, \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}),
\]
natural in $\mathcal{L}$ and $\enriched{B}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
Concretely, the free $\mathcal{V}$-category $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}$ has the objects of
$\mathcal{L}$ and hom-objects
$\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}(K,L) = \mathop{\bigcoprod}\limits_{u \in \mathcal{L}(K,L)} \I_{\mathcal{V}}$, with
injections written $\iota_{u}$, and the unit of the adjunction is the identity-on-objects
functor
\[
\psi_{\mathcal{L}} : \mathcal{L} \to \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L},
\qquad u \longmapsto \iota_{u}.
\]
A functor $T : \mathcal{L} \to \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}$ names, for each
$u \in \mathcal{L}(K,L)$, a morphism $T(u) : \I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{B}(TK,TL)$, and its
transpose $\overline{T} : \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L} \to \enriched{B}$ is given on objects by
$\overline{T}K = TK$ and on hom-objects by the copairing
\[
\overline{T}_{K,L} \defeq [\,T(u)\,]_{u \in \mathcal{L}(K,L)} :
\bigcoprod_{u \in \mathcal{L}(K,L)} \I_{\mathcal{V}} \longrightarrow \enriched{B}(TK,TL).
\]
The transpose of a natural transformation $\alpha : T \Rightarrow S$ is the
$\mathcal{V}$-natural $\overline{\alpha} : \overline{T} \Rightarrow \overline{S}$ with the same
components, each $\alpha_{K} \in \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}(TK,SK)$ being already a morphism
$\I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{B}(TK,SK)$ in $\mathcal{V}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{remark:underlying_action_on_functors}
The action of $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}$ on a $\mathcal{V}$-functor $T : \enriched{C} \to \enriched{D}$ is given by map
\[
(\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}T)_{A,B} = \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(T_{A,B}) : \mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, \enriched{C}(A,B)) \to \mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, \enriched{D}(TA,TB))
\]
which sends a morphism $f : \I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{C}(A,B)$ to $T_{A,B} \circ f : \I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{D}(TA,TB)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
Let $\mathcal{L}$ be an ordinary category and $\enriched{B}$ an $\catname{SLat}$-category.
By~\cref{rem:slat-free-concrete}, the hom-objects of the free
$\catname{SLat}$-category are
\[
\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}\mathcal{L}(K,L) = \bigcoprod_{u \in \mathcal{L}(K,L)} \I_{\catname{SLat}},
\]
the semilattice of finite non-empty subsets of $\mathcal{L}(K,L)$ under union, with the
injection $\iota_{u}$ picking out the singleton $\{u\}$, so that
$\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\} = \{u_{1}\} \join \ldots \join \{u_{n}\}$.

A functor $T : \mathcal{L} \to \widetilde{U}_{\catname{SLat}}\enriched{B}$ interprets each morphism
$u : K \to L$ of $\mathcal{L}$ as an element $T(u)$ of the hom-semilattice
$\enriched{B}(TK,TL)$, that is, as a morphism
$T(u) : \I_{\catname{SLat}} \to \enriched{B}(TK,TL)$.
Its transpose $\overline{T} : \widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}\mathcal{L} \to \enriched{B}$ agrees with $T$ on
objects and on singletons, and is therefore given by
\[
\overline{T}(\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}) = T(u_{1}) \join \ldots \join T(u_{n}).
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:free-enrichment-monoidal}
Let $(\mathcal{C}, \otimes_{\mathcal{C}}, I_{\mathcal{C}})$ be an ordinary monoidal category and let
$F : \catname{Set} \to \mathcal{V}$ be the free functor of~\cref{prop:2-adjunction}, which is
symmetric monoidal by~\cref{cor:cocomplete_monoidal_free}.
Write $\enriched{C} \defeq \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})$.
By~\cref{prop:tensor_preserved_by_enrichment}, the monoidal structure of $\mathcal{C}$ lifts to $\enriched{C}$; we
record what it looks like concretely.

The tensor product $\otimes_{\mathcal{C}}$ is given by a bifunctor
\begin{gather*}
\otimes_{\mathcal{C}} : \mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{C}\\
(A,B) \longmapsto A \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} B\\
(f,g) \longmapsto f \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} g
\end{gather*}
The ordinary product category $\mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{C}$ is carried to the tensor
$\mathcal{V}$-category $\enriched{C} \boxtimes \enriched{C}$.
Both have as objects the pairs $\obj{\mathcal{C}} \times \obj{\mathcal{C}}$, and on hom-objects
\begin{align*}
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{C})\big((A,B),(C,D)\big)
&= F\big(\mathcal{C}(A,C) \times \mathcal{C}(B,D)\big)\\
&\cong F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(A,C)) \otimes F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(B,D))\\
&= (\enriched{C} \boxtimes \enriched{C})\big((A,B),(C,D)\big),
\end{align*}
the middle isomorphism being the structure map of the strong monoidal $F_{\mathcal{V}}$ (~\cref{cor:cocomplete_monoidal_free}).
Its coherence makes this isomorphism compatible with composition and identities, so it is an
isomorphism of $\mathcal{V}$-categories
$\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C} \times \mathcal{C}) \cong \enriched{C} \boxtimes \enriched{C}$.

The monoidal structure on $\enriched{C}$ is then the $\mathcal{V}$-functor
$\enriched{\otimes} \defeq \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\otimes_{\mathcal{C}})$, acting on objects by
$(A,B) \mapsto A \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} B$ and on hom-objects by
\[
\enriched{\otimes}_{(A,B),(C,D)} :
(\bigcoprod_{\mathcal{C}(A,C)} \I_{\mathcal{V}}) \otimes (\bigcoprod_{\mathcal{C}(B,D)} \I_{\mathcal{V}})
\;\cong\;
\bigcoprod_{\mathcal{C}(A,C) \times \mathcal{C}(B,D)} \I_{\mathcal{V}}
\;\xrightarrow{\ F_{\mathcal{V}}(\otimes_{\mathcal{C}})\ }
\bigcoprod_{\mathcal{C}(A \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} B,\, C \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} D)} \I_{\mathcal{V}},
\]
the unit being the $\mathcal{V}$-functor $\enriched{I} \to \enriched{C}$ selecting
$I_{\mathcal{C}}$.
A map out of a coproduct is determined by its components, so
$\enriched{\otimes}_{(A,B),(C,D)}$ is the copairing whose component at
$(f,g) \in \mathcal{C}(A,C) \times \mathcal{C}(B,D)$ is the injection
$\iota_{f \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} g}$.
In other words, $\enriched{\otimes}$ is the unique extension of $\otimes_{\mathcal{C}}$ to the
structure freely added by $F$: it is forced on generators, and determined everywhere else.

The associator, unitors and symmetry of $\mathcal{C}$ are invertible $2$-cells in
$\catname{Cat}$, so $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}$ carries them to invertible $\mathcal{V}$-natural
transformations, and the pentagon, triangle and hexagon axioms transport because a $2$-functor
preserves commuting diagrams of $2$-cells.
\end{example}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:2-adjunction-monoidal}
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be as in~\cref{prop:2-adjunction}.
The $2$-adjunction $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}} \dashv \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}$ restricts to a $2$-adjunction
\[
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}, \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}} :
\catname{SMonCat} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{SMonCat}
\qquad \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}} \dashv \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}~,
\]
where the $2$-categories have symmetric monoidal ($\mathcal{V}$-)categories as objects,
strong symmetric monoidal ($\mathcal{V}$-)functors as $1$-cells, and monoidal
($\mathcal{V}$-)natural transformations as $2$-cells.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For the objects, $F_{\mathcal{V}}$ is symmetric monoidal
by~\cref{cor:cocomplete_monoidal_free} and $U_{\mathcal{V}}$ is lax symmetric
monoidal by~\cref{remark:U_lax_monoidal}. Hence, by
\cref{prop:tensor_preserved_by_enrichment}, both
$\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}$ and $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}$ carry
symmetric monoidal ($\mathcal{V}$-)categories to symmetric monoidal
($\mathcal{V}$-)categories.

It remains to show that the transposition of
\cref{prop:2-adjunction} preserves strong symmetric monoidal functors and
monoidal natural transformations.

Let
\[
T : \mathcal{L} \longrightarrow
\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}
\]
be a strong symmetric monoidal functor, with structure isomorphisms
\[
T_{2}^{A,B} :
TA \otimes_{\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}} TB \longrightarrow T(A \otimes_{\mathcal{L}} B),
\qquad
T_{0} :
I_{\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}} \longrightarrow T(I_{\mathcal{L}}).
\]
Its transpose
\[
\overline{T} :
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}
\longrightarrow \enriched{B}
\]
has the same action as $T$ on objects. 
Write
\[
\enriched{C}\defeq\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}.
\]
The tensor comparison for $\overline{T}$ should be a
$\mathcal{V}$-natural transformation
\[
\overline{T}_{2} :
\overline{\otimes}_{\enriched{B}}
\circ
(\overline{T}\boxtimes\overline{T})
\Longrightarrow
\overline{T}
\circ
\overline{\otimes}_{\enriched{C}},
\]
between $\mathcal{V}$-functors
$\enriched{C}\boxtimes\enriched{C}\to\enriched{B}$.
Its component at $(A,B)$ must be a morphism
\[
\overline{T}_{2}^{A,B} :
\I_{\mathcal{V}}
\longrightarrow
\enriched{B}\bigl(
  \overline{T}A
    \overline{\otimes}_{\enriched{B}}
  \overline{T}B,\,
  \overline{T}
    (A\overline{\otimes}_{\enriched{C}}B)
\bigr).
\]
The transpose $\overline{T}$ has the same action on objects as $T$, and
the tensor product of $\enriched{C}$ has the same action on objects as
that of $\mathcal{L}$. Thus this is equivalently a morphism
\[
\overline{T}_{2}^{A,B} :
\I_{\mathcal{V}}
\longrightarrow
\enriched{B}\bigl(
  TA\otimes_{\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}}TB,\,
  T(A\otimes_{\mathcal{L}}B)
\bigr).
\]
By the definition of the underlying category, the original comparison
isomorphism
\[
T_{2}^{A,B} :
TA\otimes_{\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}}TB
\longrightarrow
T(A\otimes_{\mathcal{L}}B)
\]
is precisely such a generalised element. We therefore define
$\overline{T}_{2}^{A,B}$ to be the generalised element corresponding to
$T_{2}^{A,B}$.

To see that these components are $\mathcal{V}$-natural, recall that
\[
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L})
\cong
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}
\boxtimes
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L} = \enriched{C} \boxtimes \enriched{C}.
\]
Its hom-objects are freely generated by the morphisms $(f,g)$ of
$\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}$. Consequently, the enriched naturality
equation need only be checked on the corresponding injections
$\iota_f\otimes\iota_g$. On such a generator it is exactly the ordinary
naturality equation
\[
T_{2}^{A',B'} \circ (T(f)\otimes T(g))
=
T(f\otimes g)\circ T_{2}^{A,B}.
\]
This holds because $T_{2}$ is natural.
The same argument applies to the unit comparison $\overline{T}_{0}$.

The associativity, unit and symmetry axioms for
$\overline{T}_{2}$ and $\overline{T}_{0}$ are equations between the same
generalised elements as the corresponding axioms for $T_{2}$ and
$T_{0}$. They therefore hold for $\overline{T}$.

Moreover, the inverses of $T_{2}^{A,B}$ and $T_{0}$ supply inverse
components for $\overline{T}_{2}$ and $\overline{T}_{0}$; their enriched
naturality follows by the same argument. Thus $\overline{T}$ is a strong
symmetric monoidal $\mathcal{V}$-functor.

Conversely, let
\[
S :
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}
\longrightarrow \enriched{B}
\]
be a strong symmetric monoidal $\mathcal{V}$-functor. Applying
$\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}$ to its structure maps gives a strong
symmetric monoidal functor
\[
\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(S) :
\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}
\longrightarrow
\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}\enriched{B}.
\]
The unit
\[
\psi_{\mathcal{L}} :
\mathcal{L}
\longrightarrow
\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}
\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{L}
\]
is strict symmetric monoidal. It is the identity on objects, and on
morphisms
\[
\psi_{\mathcal{L}}(f\otimes g)
=
\iota_{f\otimes g}
=
\iota_f\otimes\iota_g
=
\psi_{\mathcal{L}}(f)\otimes\psi_{\mathcal{L}}(g);
\]
the unit object and symmetries are preserved in the same way.
It follows that
\[
\Xi(S)
=
\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(S)\circ\psi_{\mathcal{L}}
\]
is strong symmetric monoidal.

Finally, transposition leaves the components of natural transformations
unchanged. Hence the equations saying that a natural transformation is
monoidal are identical on the two sides. Thus $\Xi$ restricts to an
isomorphism between the hom-categories of strong symmetric monoidal
functors and monoidal natural transformations. Since the original
isomorphism is natural, these restricted isomorphisms define the stated
$2$-adjunction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a symmetric monoidal closed category, with the closed structure given by the adjunction
\[
- \otimes X, [X,-] \colon \mathcal{V} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{V} \qquad - \otimes X \dashv [X,-],
\]
and counit of the adjunction, written $\mathrm{ev}$, being the evaluation map
\begin{gather*}
\mathrm{ev} : [X,-] \otimes X \Rightarrow \mathrm{Id}\\
\mathrm{ev}_{Y} : [X,Y] \otimes X \Rightarrow Y~.
\end{gather*}
Or, equivalently, as a natural isomorphism
\[
\mathcal{V}(A \otimes X, B) \cong \mathcal{V}(A, [X,B])~. \tag{$\ast$}
\]
% Substituting $X = \I_{\mathcal{V}}$ in the above isomorphism, we get $\mathcal{V}(A,B) \cong \mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, [\I_{\mathcal{V}},B])$ and thus $B \cong [\I_{\mathcal{V}}, B]$ by Yoneda.
% Write $i_{B} : B \to [\I_{\mathcal{V}}, B]$ for this isomorphism.
Then, $\mathcal{V}$ can be considered as a $\mathcal{V}$-category $\enriched{V}$, being enriched in itself.
Hom-objects $\enriched{V}(A,B)$ are given by the internal homs $[A,B]$ with the composition given by the transpose under the adjunction of the following composite
\[
[B,C] \otimes ([A,B] \otimes A) \xrightarrow{\id \otimes \mathrm{ev}_{B}} [B,C] \otimes B \xrightarrow{\mathrm{ev}_{C}} C~.
\] 
The identity is given by the transpose of the following composite
\[
\I_{\mathcal{V}} \otimes A \xrightarrow{\cong} A~.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
We have that $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(\enriched{V}) \cong \mathcal{V}$.
By definition, $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(\enriched{V})$ has the same objects as $\enriched{V}$, i.e., those of $\mathcal{V}$.
The hom-sets of $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(\enriched{V})$ are given by $U_{\mathcal{V}}(\enriched{V}(A,B)) = \mathcal{V}(\I_{\mathcal{V}}, [A,B]) \cong \mathcal{V}(A,B)$.
\end{remark}

This allows us to formulate hom-object $\mathcal{V}$-functors.
\begin{definition}[{\cite[Sec. 1.6]{Kelly2022BASICCO}}]\label{def:enriched-hom-functor}
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be symmetric monoidal closed and $\enriched{C}$ a $\mathcal{V}$-category.
The \emph{hom-object functor} of $\enriched{C}$ is the $\mathcal{V}$-functor
\[
\enriched{C}(-,-) : \enriched{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \boxtimes \enriched{C}
\longrightarrow \enriched{V}, \qquad (A,B) \longmapsto \enriched{C}(A,B),
\]
whose action on hom-objects
\[
\enriched{C}(B,A) \otimes \enriched{C}(C,D) \longrightarrow
[\,\enriched{C}(A,C),\, \enriched{C}(B,D)\,]
\]
is the transpose, under the adjunction $- \otimes X \dashv [X,-]$, of the map 
\begin{align*}
\enriched{C}(B,A) \otimes \enriched{C}(C,D) \otimes \enriched{C}(A,C)
&\xrightarrow{\ \sym \otimes \id\ }
\enriched{C}(C,D) \otimes \enriched{C}(B,A) \otimes \enriched{C}(A,C)\\
&\xrightarrow{\ \id \otimes \sym\ }
\enriched{C}(C,D) \otimes \enriched{C}(A,C) \otimes \enriched{C}(B,A)\\
&\xrightarrow{\ \id \otimes \circ_{B,A,C}\ }
\enriched{C}(C,D) \otimes \enriched{C}(B,C)\\
&\xrightarrow{\ \circ_{B,C,D}\ }
\enriched{C}(B,D)~,
\end{align*}
built from the composition of $\enriched{C}$ and the symmetry of $\mathcal{V}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
With $\mathcal{V}$ viewed as a $\mathcal{V}$-category $\enriched{V}$, we can equivalently formulate the adjunction as a $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism between hom-objects.
Recall $\eta$, $\xi$ and $H : \enriched{D} \to \enriched{C}$, $G : \enriched{C} \to \enriched{D}$ from~\cref{def:enriched-adjunction}.
Then, the hom-object counterpart to the above definition can be derived as usual by the following two composites.
\[
\enriched{C}(HA, B) \cong \enriched{C}(HA, B) \otimes \I_{\mathcal{V}} \xrightarrow{G_{HA,B} \otimes \eta_{A}} \enriched{D}(GHA, GB) \otimes \enriched{D}(A, GHA) \xrightarrow{\circ_{A,GHA,GB}} \enriched{D}(A, GB)~, 
\]
\[
\enriched{D}(A, GB) \cong \I_{\mathcal{V}} \otimes \enriched{D}(A, GB) \xrightarrow{\xi_{B} \otimes  H_{A, GB}} \enriched{C}(HGB, B) \otimes \enriched{C}(HA, HGB) \xrightarrow{\circ_{HA,HGB,B}} \enriched{C}(HA, B)~. 
\]
These composites are mutually inverse and constitute a $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism between the hom-objects.
$\mathcal{V}$-structure on $\enriched{V}$ is required to recognise $\enriched{C}(H-,-)$ and $\enriched{D}(-, G-)$ as $\mathcal{V}$-functors valued in $\enriched{V}$.
This yields the familiar formulation
\[
\enriched{C}(HA, B) \cong \enriched{D}(A, GB)~.
\]
\end{remark}

It is easy to see that the free enrichment functor $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}$ preserves adjunctions by acting on the natural transformations $\eta$ and $\xi$: since functors preserve composition, the commutative diagrams of the adjunction stay intact.
Therefore, it is more instructive to note how the hom-object formulation of an adjunction is transformed by $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:free-enrichment-adjunction}
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a symmetric monoidal closed cocomplete category.
Let $\mathcal{C}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ be ordinary categories and let
\[
H, G : \mathcal{D} \rightleftarrows \mathcal{C} \qquad H \dashv G
\]
be an adjunction, with unit $\eta$, counit $\xi$, and natural isomorphism of hom-sets
$\Psi_{A,B} : \mathcal{C}(HA,B) \cong \mathcal{D}(A,GB)$.
Write $\enriched{C} = \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C})$,
$\enriched{D} = \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{D})$,
$\enriched{H} = \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(H)$ and
$\enriched{G} = \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(G)$.

Since $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \times \mathcal{C})
= \enriched{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \boxtimes \enriched{C}$ by~\cref{ex:free-enrichment-monoidal} and~\cref{def:tensor_category}, and since $\mathcal{V}$ is the underlying ordinary
category of $\enriched{V}$, the ordinary functors
\[
F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{C}(H-,-), \quad
F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{D}(-,G-) \;:\;
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \times \mathcal{C} \longrightarrow \mathcal{V}
\]
transpose under~\cref{prop:2-adjunction} to $\mathcal{V}$-functors
$\enriched{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \boxtimes \enriched{C} \to \enriched{V}$, which are the enriched
hom-functors $\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}-,-)$ and $\enriched{D}(-,\enriched{G}-)$ of~\cref{def:enriched-hom-functor}.
Write $F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi$ for the natural transformation obtained by applying
$F_{\mathcal{V}}$ to each component of $\Psi$, that is,
$(F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi)_{A,B} = F_{\mathcal{V}}(\Psi_{A,B})$.
Then $\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi}$, the transpose of $F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi$, is a
$\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}A, B) \;\cong\; \enriched{D}(A, \enriched{G}B)~,
\]
and it agrees with the isomorphism determined by the $\mathcal{V}$-adjunction
$\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\eta)$, $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\xi)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First, $F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi$ is a natural isomorphism because composing a functor with a natural isomorphism preserves naturality and isomorphisms.
Then, by~\cref{prop:2-adjunction}, the transpose $\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi}$ is a $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism.
We need to check that transpose of $F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{C}(H-,-)$ is the enriched hom-functor $\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}-,-)$.
The transpose $\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{C}(H-,-)}$ has the defining property of $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{C}(H-,-)}) \circ \psi_{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \times \mathcal{C}} = F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{C}(H-,-)$.
We will show that $\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}-,-)$ satisfies the same property, that is that $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}-,-)) \circ \psi_{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \times \mathcal{C}} = F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{C}(H-,-)$, which will establish the equality of the two functors.

The functors $\widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}$ and $\psi$ are identity-on-objects, so it suffices to check that $\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}-,-)$ and $F_{\mathcal{V}} \circ \mathcal{C}(H-,-)$ agree on objects.
On objects, $\enriched{H}$ agrees with $H$ and, by change of base,
$\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}A, C) = F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(HA,C))$, which is the value of the
right-hand side at $(A,C)$.

On morphisms, let $(f,g) : (A,C) \to (B,D)$ in $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \times \mathcal{C}$,
so $f : B \to A$ in $\mathcal{D}$ and $g : C \to D$ in $\mathcal{C}$.
The right-hand side gives $F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(Hf,g))$.
For the left-hand side, $\psi$ sends $(f,g)$ to the injection $\iota_{(f,g)}$, and the
underlying functor of the enriched hom-functor sends it to the morphism
\[
\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}A, C) \longrightarrow \enriched{C}(\enriched{H}B, D)
\]
of $\mathcal{V}$ given by precomposition with $\enriched{H}(\iota_{f}) = \iota_{Hf}$ and
postcomposition with $\iota_{g}$ as per~\cref{def:enriched-hom-functor} and~\cref{remark:underlying_action_on_functors}.
Under the identification $\widetilde{F}(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \times \mathcal{C}) = \enriched{D}^{\mathrm{op}} \boxtimes \enriched{C}$, given by the structure map $\Phi$ of the strong monoidal $F_{\mathcal{V}}$, the injection $\iota_{(f,g)}$ corresponds to $\iota_{f} \otimes \iota_{g}$.

Both are morphisms out of $\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}A,C) = \bigcoprod_{h} \I_{\mathcal{V}}$, so
it suffices to compare them on the injections $\iota_{h}$, for
$h \in \mathcal{C}(HA,C)$.
The right-hand side gives
$F_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{C}(Hf,g)) \circ \iota_{h} = \iota_{g \circ h \circ Hf}$,
since $F_{\mathcal{V}}(\varphi) \circ \iota_{x} = \iota_{\varphi(x)}$.
The left-hand side gives the same, since composition in $\enriched{C} = \widetilde{F}\mathcal{C}$
is $F_{\mathcal{V}}(\circ_{\mathcal{C}})$ after the structure map $\Phi$, which carries
injections to injections, so $\iota_{g} \circ \iota_{h} \circ \iota_{Hf}
= \iota_{g \circ h \circ Hf}$.
The two functors therefore agree after composing with $\psi$, and therefore are equal, since the transpose is unique.
A similar argument applies to the other functor.

It remains to check that $\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi}_{A,B}$ agrees with the composite 
\[
\Theta = \circ_{A, \enriched{G}\enriched{H}A, \enriched{G}B} \circ (\enriched{G}_{\enriched{H}A,B} \otimes \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\eta)_{A}) \circ \rho^{-1}_{\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}A,B)}~,
\] 
recalling that $\Psi_{A,B}(h) = G(h) \circ \eta_{A}$.
These are maps out of $\enriched{C}(\enriched{H}A,B) = \bigcoprod_{h \in \mathcal{C}(HA,B)} \I_{\mathcal{V}}$, so it suffices to check them on the injections $\iota_{h}$, for $h \in \mathcal{C}(HA,B)$.
Precomposing with $\iota_{h}$, the left-hand side gives
$\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi}_{A,B} \circ \iota_{h} = \iota_{\Psi_{A,B}(h)}
= \iota_{G(h) \circ \eta_{A}}$,
since the transpose has components $F_{\mathcal{V}}(\Psi_{A,B})$ and
$F_{\mathcal{V}}(\varphi) \circ \iota_{x} = \iota_{\varphi(x)}$.

For the right-hand side, $\rho^{-1} \circ \iota_{h} = \iota_{h} \otimes \id_{\I_{\mathcal{V}}}$
modulo the unitor, so the middle map sends it to
$\enriched{G}_{\enriched{H}A,B}(\iota_{h}) \otimes \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\eta)_{A}$.
Now $\enriched{G} = \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(G)$ acts on hom-objects as
$F_{\mathcal{V}}(G_{-,-})$, so $\enriched{G}_{\enriched{H}A,B}(\iota_{h}) = \iota_{G(h)}$, and
the components of $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\eta)$ are the injections at the components of
$\eta$, so $\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\eta)_{A} = \iota_{\eta_{A}}$.
Finally, composition in the free enrichment $\enriched{D} = \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{D})$
is $F_{\mathcal{V}}(\circ_{\mathcal{D}})$ after the structure map, which carries injections to
injections, so
\[
\circ_{A,\,\enriched{G}\enriched{H}A,\,\enriched{G}B}
\big(\iota_{G(h)} \otimes \iota_{\eta_{A}}\big) = \iota_{G(h) \circ \eta_{A}}~.
\]
The two agree on every injection, hence $\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi}_{A,B} = \Theta_{A,B}$.
Since $A$ and $B$ were arbitrary, $\overline{F_{\mathcal{V}}\Psi} = \Theta$, so the isomorphism
obtained by transposing $\Psi$ is the one determined by the $\mathcal{V}$-adjunction
$\widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(H) \dashv \widetilde{F}_{\mathcal{V}}(G)$.
\end{proof}

Even though the free enrichment functor $\widetilde{F}$ preserves adjunctions, in general it does not guarantee that the translated adjunction corresponds to the same limit or colimit, although the latter is better behaved if the monoidal structure of the base for enrichment is cartesian.
To illustrate this, we need to digress into a discussion of limits in a $\mathcal{V}$-category.
First recall the definition of an ordinary categorical limit in a 1-category.
\begin{definition}[Limit]
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a category and let $D : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ be a functor, also called a diagram.
Let $L$ be an object of $\mathcal{C}$ and let $\Delta L : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ be the constant functor that maps every object of $\mathcal{J}$ to $L$ and every morphism of $\mathcal{J}$ to $\id_{L}$.
A cone with apex $L$ over $D$ is a natural transformation $\lambda : \Delta L \to D$, with the component at $j$ being in $\mathcal{C}(L,D(j))$.
Such natural transformations form a set $\mathrm{Cone}(\Delta L,D)$.
A limit of $D$ is then an object $\lim D$ such that there exists an isomorphism $\mathcal{C}(L, \lim D) \cong \mathrm{Cone}(\Delta L, D)$, natural in $L$.

The set of these cones $\mathrm{Cone}(\Delta L,D)$ can be equivalently expressed as a limit in $\catname{Set}$, by noting that a functor $\mathcal{C}(L,D-)$ takes each $j \in \mathcal{J}$ to the set $\mathcal{C}(L,D(j))$ and each morphism $f : j \to k$ to $\mathcal{C}(L,Dj) \to \mathcal{C}(L,Dk)$ given by $Df \circ -$. 
Take $\mathfrak{1}_{\catname{Set}}$ as the apex of the cone for $\mathcal{C}(L,D-)$, then it picks a single morphism $h_{j}$ out of each $\mathcal{C}(L,D(j))$, and the naturality condition at $f : j \to k$ says that $Df \circ h_{j} = h_{k}$ holds iff the family $(h_{j})_{j}$ forms a cone with apex $L$, so cones over $D$ with the apex $L$ are in one-to-one correspondence with cones over $\mathcal{C}(L,D-)$ with the apex $\mathfrak{1}_{\catname{Set}}$.
By the definition of a limit, we have that $\catname{Set}(S, \lim \mathcal{C}(L,D-)) \cong \mathrm{Cone}(\Delta S, \mathcal{C}(L,D-))$ and in particular we have $\catname{Set}(\mathfrak{1}_{\catname{Set}}, \lim \mathcal{C}(L,D-)) \cong \mathrm{Cone}(\Delta \mathfrak{1}_{\catname{Set}}, \mathcal{C}(L,D-)) \cong \mathrm{Cone}(\Delta L,D)$ and therefore $\mathrm{Cone}(\Delta L,D) \cong \lim \mathcal{C}(L,D-)$ since $\catname{Set}(\mathfrak{1}_{\catname{Set}}, X) \cong X$.
So we can equivalently say that $\lim D$ is an object such that 
\[
\mathcal{C}(L, \lim D) \cong \lim \mathcal{C}(L,D-),
\]
natural in $L$, and the latter is a limit in $\catname{Set}$.
That is, an arbitrary limit in a category $\mathcal{C}$ can be characterised as a limit in $\catname{Set}$; this principle will be essential when generalising to enriched categories.
\end{definition}

For our running example of $\catname{SLat}$-categories, we will be interested in products and terminal objects, so let us recall these notions as limits in an ordinary category.
\begin{definition}
The product of two objects $A$ and $B$ in $\mathcal{C}$ is an object $A \times B$, which is a limit of a diagram $D : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ where $\mathcal{J}$ has two objects $1$ and $2$ and no non-identity morphisms, so $D(1) = A$ and $D(2) = B$.
Applying the above definition, this amounts to the isomorphism in $\catname{Set}$, natural in $X$
\[
\mathcal{C}(X, A \times B) \cong \lim_{i \in \mathcal{J}} \mathcal{C}(X, D(i)).
\]
The right-hand side is a product in $\catname{Set}$ for the diagram $\mathcal{C}(X,D-)$ and thus
\[
\mathcal{C}(X, A \times B) \cong \mathcal{C}(X, A) \times \mathcal{C}(X,B).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
The terminal object $\mathfrak{1}_{\mathcal{C}}$ of $\mathcal{C}$ is the limit of the empty diagram $D : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ where $\mathcal{J}$ has no objects.
As above, it is characterised by the terminal object in $\catname{Set}$, and thus
\[
\mathcal{C}(X, \mathfrak{1}_{\mathcal{C}}) \cong \mathfrak{1}_\catname{Set} \cong \I_{\catname{Set}},
\]
natural in $X$.
\end{definition}

Before we generalise the above discussion to enriched categories, let us note that the traditional definition of a limit using limiting cones cannot be directly generalised to the enriched setting.
Such a definition requires a notion of a constant functor $\Delta L$, which in the unenriched case sends every object of $\mathcal{J}$ to $L \in \obj{\mathcal{C}}$ and every morphism to $\id_{L}$.
Mapping objects when $\enriched{J}$ and $\enriched{C}$ are $\mathcal{V}$-categories is no issue, however, the action of $\Delta$ on hom-objects must then be $\Delta_{X,Y} : \enriched{J}(X,Y) \to \enriched{C}(L,L)$, such that it factors as $\enriched{J}(X,Y) \to \I_{\mathcal{V}} \xrightarrow{j_{L}} \enriched{C}(L,L)$.
The maps $\enriched{J}(X,Y) \to \I_{\mathcal{V}}$ must furthermore be compatible with composition and identities, thus amounting to a $\mathcal{V}$-functor $\enriched{J} \to \enriched{I}$ to the unit $\mathcal{V}$-category~(\cref{def:tensor_category}).
Such a functor exists when $\enriched{I}$ is also a terminal category in $\mathcal{V}\text{-}\catname{Cat}$, but may not exist in general.

Now we have all the ingredients to define limits in a $\mathcal{V}$-category.
The correct generalisation proceeds by considering two $\mathcal{V}$-functors $W : \enriched{J} \to \enriched{V}$ and $G : \enriched{J} \to \enriched{C}$, with $W$ being called a \emph{weight} and $G$ being a diagram.
The apex of a cone is then an object $X$ of $\enriched{C}$ and all cones are given by the object of natural transformations $\enriched{C}(X, G-)^{W}$ as per~\cref{def:enriched_natural_object}.
Each leg of the cone is then given by the hom-object $[WK, \enriched{C}(X, GK)]$, roughly an object of morphisms $X \to GK$ indexed by $WK$, i.e., each leg potentially contains a whole family of morphisms in the enriched case.
The limit of $G$ weighted by $W$, written $\{W,G\}$ is then given by the $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\enriched{C}(X, \{W,G\}) \cong [\enriched{J}, \enriched{V}](W, \enriched{C}(X, G-))~,
\]
where $[\enriched{J}, \enriched{V}]$ is the $\mathcal{V}$-category of $\mathcal{V}$-functors: the objects are $\mathcal{V}$-functors and hom-objects are objects of $\mathcal{V}$-natural transformations as in~\cref{def:enriched_natural_object}.
We will not need the full generality of weighted limits and we will focus on the special case of \emph{conical limits} below.

\begin{definition}[Conical limit~{\cite[Sec. 3.8]{Kelly2022BASICCO}}]
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a complete and cocomplete symmetric monoidal closed category.
Let $\enriched{C}$ be a $\mathcal{V}$-category and let $\mathcal{J}$ be an ordinary $\catname{Set}$-category.
Let $\mathcal{C} = \widetilde{U}(\enriched{C})$ be the underlying ordinary category of $\enriched{C}$ and let $D : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ be a functor.
A \emph{conical} limit of $D$ is an object $\lim D$ such that there is a $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\enriched{C}(X, \lim D) \cong \lim_{i \in \mathcal{J}} \enriched{C}(X, D(i))~,
\]
with the limit on the right-hand side being the usual limit in $\mathcal{V}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
The above definition can be seen as an instance of a weighted limit, by taking the weight $W : \enriched{J} \to \enriched{V}$, with $\enriched{J} \defeq \widetilde{F}(\mathcal{J})$, to be the transpose of the constant functor $\Delta\I : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{V}$ under the adjunction of~\cref{prop:2-adjunction}.
The diagram $G : \enriched{J} \to \enriched{C}$ is then obtained as a transpose of $D : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ along the same adjunction.
By the definition of a weighted limit above, we have that
\[
\enriched{C}(X, \{W,G\}) \cong [\enriched{J}, \enriched{V}](W, \enriched{C}(X, G-))~.
\]
The conical limit, on the other hand, is given by
\begin{align}\label{eq:conical_limit_remark}
\enriched{C}(X, \lim D) \cong \lim_{i \in \mathcal{J}} \enriched{C}(X, D(i))~.
\end{align}
Both constructions on the right-hand sides are characterised as equalizers: the first being the definition of the object of natural transformations as per~\cref{def:enriched_natural_object}, while the other being the usual limit in $\mathcal{V}$ seen as an equalizer, so we just need to conclude that the equalizers are isomorphic.

Let us now look at the product and terminal object in a $\mathcal{V}$-category as instances of conical limits.
\begin{example}
Let $\mathcal{J}$ be a category with two objects $1$ and $2$ and no non-identity morphisms and let $\enriched{C}$ be a $\mathcal{V}$-category.
Let $\mathcal{C} = \widetilde{U}_{\mathcal{V}}(\enriched{C})$.
The diagram for a product is then given by a functor $D : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ mapping $1$ to $A$ and $2$ to $B$ and $\id_{1}$ and $\id_{2}$ to $j_{A} : \I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{C}(A,A)$ and $j_{B} : \I_{\mathcal{V}} \to \enriched{C}(B,B)$, respectively.
By definition, we get that the product of $A$ and $B$ is an object $A \times B$ such that the following is a $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\enriched{C}(L, A \times B) \cong \enriched{C}(L, A) \times \enriched{C}(L, B) \tag{$\ast$}~,
\]
with the product on the right-hand side being taken in $\mathcal{V}$.

Now suppose $\mathcal{V} = \catname{SLat}$, then the product is characterised by a product in $\catname{SLat}$ (in the sense of~\cref{remark:slat-limits}).
Let the $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism in $(\ast)$ be given by $\catname{SLat}$-natural transformation $\Psi$.
Then, projections are given by the composite
\[
\I_{\catname{SLat}} \xrightarrow{j_{A \times B}} \enriched{C}(A \times B, A \times B) \xrightarrow{\Psi_{A \times B}} \enriched{C}(A \times B, A) \times \enriched{C}(A \times B, B)~,
\]
postcomposed with either $\pi_{1,\mathcal{V}}$ or $\pi_{2,\mathcal{V}}$ to yield $\pi_{1} : \I_{\catname{SLat}} \to \enriched{C}(A \times B, A)$ or $\pi_{2} : \I_{\catname{SLat}} \to \enriched{C}(A \times B, B)$, respectively.
This does not equip the projections with any extra properties beyond those given by the composition in $\catname{SLat}$, e.g., that $\pi_{1} \circ (f \join g) = \pi_{1} \circ f \join \pi_{1} \circ g$.

Pairing is then given by the following composite
\[
\I_{\catname{SLat}} \xrightarrow{\langle f, g \rangle_{\mathcal{V}}} \enriched{C}(L, A) \times \enriched{C}(L,B) \xrightarrow{\Psi_{L}^{-1}} \enriched{C}(L, A \times B)~,
\]
where $\langle f, g \rangle_{\mathcal{V}}$ is the pairing in $\catname{SLat}$ with $f$ and $g$ viewed as generalised elements of $\enriched{C}(L, A)$ and $\enriched{C}(L, B)$.
Recall that elements of $\enriched{C}(L, A) \times \enriched{C}(L,B)$ are given by pairs $(u,v)$ such that $(u_1 \join u_2, v_1 \join v_2) = (u_1, v_1) \join (u_2, v_2)$.
We then have the following property
\begin{align*}
\langle f_1 \join f_2, g_1 \join g_2 \rangle &= \Psi_{L}^{-1}(f_1 \join f_2, g_1 \join g_2)\quad\text{ by definition of $\langle -, - \rangle$}\\
&= \Psi_{L}^{-1}((f_1, g_1) \join (f_2, g_2))\quad\text{by definition of a product in $\catname{SLat}$}\\
&= \Psi_{L}^{-1}(f_1, g_1) \join \Psi_{L}^{-1}(f_2, g_2)\quad \text{by $\Psi_{L}^{-1}$ being a homomorphism}\\
&= \langle f_1, g_1 \rangle \join \langle f_2, g_2 \rangle 
\end{align*}
Remembering that a product of two morphisms $f : A \to B$ and $g : C \to D$, $f \times g : A \times C \to B \times D$ is given by the pairing $\langle f \circ \pi_{1}, g \circ \pi_{2} \rangle$, 
we have, in particular, that 
\begin{align*}
(f_1 \join f_2) \times (g_1 \join g_2) &= \langle (f_1 \join f_2) \circ \pi_{1}, (g_1 \join g_2) \circ \pi_{2} \rangle\\
&= \langle f_1 \circ \pi_{1} \join f_2 \circ \pi_{1}, g_1 \circ \pi_{2} \join g_2 \circ \pi_{2} \rangle\\
&= \langle f_1 \circ \pi_{1}, g_1 \circ \pi_{2} \rangle \join \langle f_2 \circ \pi_{1}, g_2 \circ \pi_{2} \rangle\\
&= (f_1 \times g_1) \join (f_2 \times g_2)
\end{align*} 
One may contrast this with the tensor product of morphisms in an $\catname{SLat}$-category, as in~\cref{ex:slat_tensor}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The terminal object is then given by a functor $D : \mathcal{J} \to \mathcal{C}$ where $\mathcal{J}$ is the empty category.
This amounts to the following $\mathcal{V}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\enriched{C}(L, \mathfrak{1}_\enriched{C}) \cong \mathfrak{1}_{\mathcal{V}},
\]
with the object on the right-hand side being the terminal object in $\mathcal{V}$.
In the case of $\mathcal{V} = \catname{SLat}$, $\I_{\catname{SLat}} = \{*\}$ is both the tensor unit and the terminal object and this is essentially the same notion as the ordinary terminal object: each hom-object $\enriched{C}(L, \mathfrak{1}_\enriched{C})$ has a single element.
Again, this does not equip the unique morphism $! : L \to \mathfrak{1}_\enriched{C}$ with any special properties beyond those given by the composition.
Combining it with the universal property of the terminal object, we have $! \circ (f \join g) =\;! \circ f \join \;! \circ g = \;! \join \;! = \;!$.
\end{example}

After this long digression, we can now state that despite the fact that the free $\catname{SLat}$-enrichment $2$-functor $\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}$ preserves adjunctions, it fails to preserve all binary products.
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:binary_products_not_preserved}
Free $\catname{SLat}$-enrichment does not preserve all binary products.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be an ordinary category with a binary product $A \times B$ given by the isomorphism $\Psi_{X}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prop_1595}
\mathcal{C}(X, A \times B) \cong \mathcal{C}(X, A) \times_{\catname{Set}} \mathcal{C}(X, B)~,
\end{equation}
natural in $X$.
Both sides of~\eqref{eq:prop_1595} are functors $\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \catname{Set}$, so as in~\cref{prop:free-enrichment-adjunction} we may whisker with $F_{\catname{SLat}}$ and transpose, producing $\overline{F_{\catname{SLat}}\Psi}$, a $\catname{SLat}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(X, A \times B) \cong \widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(X, A) \otimes_{\catname{SLat}} \widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(X, B)~.
\]
Concretely, the left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:prop_1595} is the representable $\mathcal{C}(-, A \times B)$, whose transpose is $\enriched{C}(-, A \times B)$, while the right-hand side is the functor $\mathcal{C}(-,A) \times_{\catname{Set}} \mathcal{C}(-,B) : \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \catname{Set}$, whose transpose is $\enriched{C}(-,A) \otimes_{\catname{SLat}} \enriched{C}(-,B) : \enriched{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \overline{\catname{SLat}}$, by strong monoidality of $F_{\catname{SLat}}$~(\cref{cor:slat-free-monoidal}).
We note that the transpose in particular turns $\times_{\catname{Set}}$ into $\otimes_{\catname{SLat}}$.
The product in a $\catname{SLat}$-category is characterised, instead, by the $\catname{SLat}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(X, A \times B) \cong \widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(X, A) \times_{\catname{SLat}} \widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(X, B)~.
\]
The latter isomorphism fails to hold in general.
For a concrete counterexample, take $\mathcal{C}=\catname{FinSet}$,
$X=1$, and $A=B=2$. There are two maps $1\to2$ and four maps
$1\to2\times2$. Since the free semilattice on an $n$-element set consists
of its non-empty subsets and hence has $2^n-1$ elements, we obtain
\[
    \left|\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(1,2\times2)\right| = 2^4-1 = 15,
    \qquad
    \left|\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(1,2) \times_{\catname{SLat}} \widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}(\mathcal{C})(1,2)\right| = (2^2-1)^2 = 9.
\]
Therefore, the two semilattices cannot be isomorphic, and $\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}$ does not carry the product cone on $A \times B$ to a product cone.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The universal property of the product in $\mathcal{C}$ is not completely lost.
Given the translated $\catname{SLat}$-isomorphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:remark_1600}
\Psi_{X} : \enriched{C}(X, A \times B) \cong \enriched{C}(X, A) \otimes_{\catname{SLat}} \enriched{C}(X, B)~,
\end{equation}
every singleton element $f$ of $\enriched{C}(X, A \times B)$ is uniquely determined by the pair of singleton elements $(\pi_{1} \circ f, \pi_{2} \circ f)$, and every pair of singleton elements $(f, g)$ uniquely determines a singleton element $\langle f, g \rangle$.
The projections are given by the composites
\begin{align*}
\I_{\catname{SLat}}
&\xrightarrow{\ j_{A \times B}\ } \enriched{C}(A \times B, A \times B)
 \xrightarrow{\ \Psi_{A \times B}\ }
 \enriched{C}(A \times B, A) \otimes \enriched{C}(A \times B, B)\\
&\cong F_{\catname{SLat}}\big(\mathcal{C}(A \times B, A) \times \mathcal{C}(A \times B, B)\big)
 \xrightarrow{\ F_{\catname{SLat}}(\pi_{1,\catname{Set}})\ }
 \enriched{C}(A \times B, A)~,\\[4pt]
\I_{\catname{SLat}}
&\xrightarrow{\ j_{A \times B}\ } \enriched{C}(A \times B, A \times B)
 \xrightarrow{\ \Psi_{A \times B}\ }
 \enriched{C}(A \times B, A) \otimes \enriched{C}(A \times B, B)\\
&\cong F_{\catname{SLat}}\big(\mathcal{C}(A \times B, A) \times \mathcal{C}(A \times B, B)\big)
 \xrightarrow{\ F_{\catname{SLat}}(\pi_{2,\catname{Set}})\ }
 \enriched{C}(A \times B, B)~.
\end{align*}
The pairing is the composite
\[
\I_{\catname{SLat}} \cong \I_{\catname{SLat}} \otimes \I_{\catname{SLat}}
\xrightarrow{\ f \otimes g\ } \enriched{C}(X, A) \otimes \enriched{C}(X, B)
\xrightarrow{\ \Psi_{X}^{-1}\ } \enriched{C}(X, A \times B)~.
\]
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}
Terminal objects are preserved by free $\catname{SLat}$-enrichment.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be an ordinary category with a terminal object $\mathfrak{1}_{\mathcal{C}}$, which amounts to the isomorphism, natural in $X$:
\[
\mathcal{C}(X, \mathfrak{1}_{\mathcal{C}}) \cong 1_{\catname{Set}}~.
\]
Both sides are functors $\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \catname{Set}$, the right-hand side being constant at the terminal set, so whiskering with $F_{\catname{SLat}}$ and transposing as in~\cref{prop:free-enrichment-adjunction} gives a $\catname{SLat}$-natural isomorphism
\[
\enriched{C}(X, \mathfrak{1}_{\mathcal{C}}) \cong F_{\catname{SLat}}(1_{\catname{Set}}) \cong \I_{\catname{SLat}} \cong \mathfrak{1}_{\catname{SLat}}~,
\]
where the middle isomorphism is the unit part of the strong monoidal structure on $F_{\catname{SLat}}$~(\cref{cor:slat-free-monoidal}), and the last holds because the one-element semilattice is both the tensor unit and the terminal object of $\catname{SLat}$.
Hence $\mathfrak{1}_{\mathcal{C}}$, which $\widetilde{F}_{\catname{SLat}}$ leaves unchanged as an object, is terminal in $\enriched{C}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The above propositions dualise to coproducts and initial objects, respectively.
\end{remark}

Non-preservation of products and preservation of terminal objects are not a bug but rather a feature of the semantic approach we take to e-graphs.
To anticipate what follows, we will consider categories with finite products that represent algebraic theories and in particular represent terms like $f(a,b)$.
Our aim is to equip these categories with a notion of e-classes, i.e., a mechanism of representing multiple equivalent terms.
The binary operation of the semilattice is our way of constructing these e-classes.
Suppose the interpretation of $f(a,b)$ is $\llbracket f \rrbracket \circ (\llbracket a \rrbracket \times \llbracket b \rrbracket)$.
Suppose our algebraic theory comes with equations $a = a'$ and $b = b'$.
If we were to represent $f(a,b)$ with these equations in an e-graph, we would have e-classes $e_1 = \{a,a'\}$ and $e_2 = \{b,b'\}$ and $e_3 = \{f(e_1,e_2)\}$.
Such an e-graph would represent the terms $f(a,b)$, $f(a',b)$, $f(a,b')$, and $f(a',b')$.
Our approach is to use $\llbracket a \rrbracket \join \llbracket a' \rrbracket$ and $\llbracket b \rrbracket \join \llbracket b' \rrbracket$ to represent these e-classes categorically.
Then, assuming the interpretation $\llbracket f \rrbracket \circ \big((\llbracket a \rrbracket \join \llbracket a' \rrbracket) \times (\llbracket b \rrbracket \join \llbracket b' \rrbracket)\big)$, we have that
\[
(\llbracket a \rrbracket \join \llbracket a' \rrbracket) \times (\llbracket b \rrbracket \join \llbracket b' \rrbracket)
= (\llbracket a \rrbracket \times \llbracket b \rrbracket) \join (\llbracket a' \rrbracket \times \llbracket b' \rrbracket)~,
\]
by the properties of a product in an $\catname{SLat}$-category (\cref{remark:slat-limits}).
Therefore, if we were to have a category with products, we would only represent $f(a,b)$ and $f(a',b')$, which is an underapproximation of what an e-graph does.
Worse, which two terms survive is not even canonically determined: since joins in a product semilattice are componentwise, the same element is equally
$(\llbracket a \rrbracket \times \llbracket b' \rrbracket) \join (\llbracket a' \rrbracket \times \llbracket b \rrbracket)$, so the pairing of the two joins is arbitrary.
If, however, we had a tensor product instead, we would have
\[
(\llbracket a \rrbracket \join \llbracket a' \rrbracket) \otimes (\llbracket b \rrbracket \join \llbracket b' \rrbracket)
= (\llbracket a \rrbracket \otimes \llbracket b \rrbracket) \join (\llbracket a \rrbracket \otimes \llbracket b' \rrbracket) \join (\llbracket a' \rrbracket \otimes \llbracket b \rrbracket) \join (\llbracket a' \rrbracket \otimes \llbracket b' \rrbracket)
\]
by~\cref{ex:slat_tensor}, and we would have all the terms.

We will then consider not only categories with finite products, but also symmetric monoidal categories and symmetric monoidal closed categories.
The results that we have summarised above, such as~\cref{prop:free-enrichment-adjunction,prop:tensor_preserved_by_enrichment}, ensure that the free $\catname{SLat}$-enrichment of these categories preserves the additional structure that we are interested in.

\paragraph{Related work.}
As noted above, this chapter recapitulates classical results of enriched
category theory and categorical algebra, specialising their constructions to
$\catname{SLat}$ and using the properties of $\catname{SLat}$ to model
equivalence classes.
The closest related work is that of Villoria et
al.~\cite{villoria_enriching_2024}, who enrich string diagrams with algebraic
operations.
Their work uses the same central device: a \emph{free enrichment}.
Whereas we present free $\mathcal{V}$-enrichment for a general base
$\mathcal{V}$ satisfying the necessary categorical assumptions, such as
cocompleteness and monoidal closedness, they take the base of enrichment to be
the category of Eilenberg--Moore algebras induced by a suitable monoidal
monad.
Their analogue of~\cref{prop:2-adjunction} is a symmetric-monoidal lift of
this adjunction, between symmetric monoidal categories and symmetric monoidal
categories enriched over the category of algebras.
Consequently, their general result encompasses both the free
$\catname{SLat}$-enrichment used here and its preservation of symmetric
monoidal structure.
The leading instance of their work is the free enrichment of the
ZX-calculus~\cite{coecke_interacting_2011} over convex algebras---the algebras
of the finite distribution monad---adding probabilistic choice; they also
consider other algebraic effects, such as multisets.
Our $\catname{SLat}$ is the Eilenberg--Moore category of the non-empty finite
powerset monad and therefore falls within their general framework.
Villoria et al. mention this monad as a possible source of nondeterministic
choice, but do not develop the corresponding $\catname{SLat}$-enrichment.

The two settings nevertheless differ in several ways.
First, we model different effects in our respective running examples: their
leading example adds probabilistic mixture, whereas ours uses semilattice join
to collect equivalent terms into an e-class.
In particular, semilattice join is idempotent and records e-class membership
rather than a weighted sum.
Second, at the categorical level relevant here, their work concentrates on
lifting symmetric monoidal structure, whereas we also study how adjunctions
and particular conical limits and colimits behave under free enrichment.
In particular, we show that free $\catname{SLat}$-enrichment preserves
terminal objects but does not in general preserve binary products, with the
dual results holding for initial objects and coproducts.
Finally, our aim is different.
We lay the semantic foundations for a fragment of e-graphs, allowing us to
generalise e-graph machinery from algebraic theories to more general monoidal
theories.
To this end, we develop e-graph-like string diagrams that may serve as a data
structure for maintaining equivalences between monoidal terms, together with
a type theory that represents such terms and their equivalences as a term
calculus. 
Our string-diagrammatic development is instead based on representing diagrams
concretely as hypergraphs, with the symmetric monoidal equations built into
this representation.
Villoria et al. do not develop their theory in this direction: in their
ZX-calculus application, enriched diagrams are treated as formal algebraic
combinations of existing ZX-diagrams, rather than being given an independent
combinatorial representation.
